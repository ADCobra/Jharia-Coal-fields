Jharia coalfield · Jharkhand, India

The ground under Jharia has been burning since 1916.

Underground coal fires crack and sink the land that people live, work and drive on. Fire Watch follows every one of them from space, and closes a road before a fire reaches it.

The mission

A fire you can't see is still a fire. Every 8 days a Landsat satellite crosses Jharia. Every fire gets followed, and every road above one gets a decision, with a person in charge whenever it matters.

  • 1916the year Jharia's coal seams were first recorded burning
  • 8 daysbetween Landsat passes over the coalfield
  • 48 dayshow far ahead each fire's spread is projected
  • 1.89 km²of ground mapped as burning by NRSC in 2020

How it works

From a satellite photo to a safer road.

Step 1 of 8

01

A satellite photographs the coalfield

Every 8 days Landsat 8 or 9 passes over Jharia. Fire Watch reads only the coalfield window of each scene: the heat band, six colour bands and the cloud mask, about 10 to 13 MB a pass.

  • Landsat Collection 2
  • ST_B10 heat band
  • QA_PIXEL cloud mask
  • USGS new-scene alerts

Output A fresh heat image and colour photo

02

Heat becomes temperature

Band 10 is turned into land-surface temperature: radiance, then brightness temperature, then a correction for how much heat the surface gives off, estimated from its vegetation. Cloud, shadow and water are masked so they can't fake a fire.

  • Brightness temperature
  • NDVI emissivity
  • Cloud, shadow & water mask

Output Surface temperature, within 0.10 K on test data

03

Every photo lined up with the last

Each pass is aligned to a reference so the same patch of ground is compared every time. SIFT aligns it, ALIKED with LightGlue on OpenCV 5's new DNN engine aligns it again on its own, and DISK settles any disagreement around a hotspot.

  • SIFT + USAC_ACCURATE
  • ALIKED + LightGlue
  • DISK
  • OpenCV 5

Output Passes aligned to within half a pixel

04

Hot ground found, false alarms filtered

Dark coal and bare dumps warm up in the sun, so that heat is regressed out against brightness and vegetation. What's left is compared with the local background, and hysteresis and contours outline each hotspot. Coke ovens and other industrial heat are recognised and ignored.

  • Albedo & NDVI regression
  • Hysteresis thresholds
  • OpenCV contours
  • Industrial mask

Output Hotspots, with precision 1.00 on both test worlds

05

Each fire followed and projected

Hotspots are joined into tracks from pass to pass. Optical flow measures how the hot front moved since the last pass, and an advection–diffusion ensemble projects where the fire could reach in the next 48 days.

  • Track matching
  • DIS optical flow
  • 48-day spread cone

Output A spread cone covering 92% of the ground a fire went on to burn

06

Risk, confidence and precedent

Every hotspot gets a risk score from its heat, its spread and how close it is to a road, plus a score for how sure the system is. Similar past fires, found with OpenCV's nearest-neighbour index, show how fires like this one behaved.

  • Risk score
  • Confidence score
  • cv2.ANNIndex precedent

Output High, medium or low risk, and how sure it is

07

The agent decides, a person stays in charge

The agent closes a road within 250 m of a fire on its own only when risk is high and it is at least 70% sure. Fires half under cloud, threats seen only in the projection and anything it can't confirm go to a person with a recommendation. After two quiet passes it proposes reopening, and only a person can reopen a road.

  • Close a road
  • Ask a person
  • Propose reopening
  • LangGraph agent

Output Matched a safety officer's call in 35 of 36 test situations

08

Everyone sees what changed

Closures and decisions appear on the dashboard and the public road-status list, with every step the agent took and why. Alerts go out through AWS, and every map layer carries a record of where it came from.

  • Dashboard
  • Public road status
  • SNS alerts
  • S3 + DynamoDB
  • XMP provenance

Output Safer roads over a burning coalfield. Read the full explanation

      About the data

      Everything on this site comes from free, public sources. Here is what they are and where they fall short.

      Satellite photos
      Landsat 8 and 9 Collection 2 Level-2 scenes (USGS), read from Microsoft Planetary Computer. Each pass brings a surface temperature image and an ordinary colour photo.
      Heat image
      Landsat measures heat at 100 m and USGS delivers it on a 30 m grid. Fire Watch looks for ground at least 8 °C hotter than its surroundings once sun-warmed coal and bare ground are accounted for.
      Roads, mines and plants
      OpenStreetMap. Roads are cut into sections of about 400 m so a closure can be precise.
      Fire area history
      NRSC fire maps for 2009, 2017 and 2020, as published by BCCL.

      What it can't do

      • Landsat passes over Jharia at about 10:15 in the morning and sees heat at the surface, not the fire itself. A deep fire can be missed.
      • Cloud hides the ground. Fire Watch waits for a clear photo instead of guessing.
      • Coal dumps and dark ground warmed by the sun can look like fire. A person reviews anything doubtful.
      • This is a research project, not an official closure notice. Follow BCCL and district instructions on the ground.
